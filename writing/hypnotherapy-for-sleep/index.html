Tired but Wired:
Hypnotherapy for Sleep

By Ashley Romero, CHt, CAHA · Published October 20, 2026

What a calmer evening can look like, what hypnotherapy can and cannot offer, and a few gentle ideas to try tonight.

It's late. You're exhausted. You have been looking forward to bed, and when your head hits the pillow your thoughts seem to switch on. If that sounds familiar, the gap between feeling tired and feeling ready to rest can be frustrating.

This is not a willpower test. Sleep is affected by many things, and one difficult night does not mean you are doing something wrong. This article is about everyday winding down, not diagnosing or treating a sleep disorder.

Why bedtime can feel like a switchboard

The day can follow us into the evening: conversations replay, tomorrow's list comes to mind, or the quiet leaves room for thoughts we could not hear earlier. A bedtime routine may help some people, but no single routine works for everyone.

It can help to notice what the transition from activity to rest looks like in your life. What tends to keep your attention? What small cue might help mark the end of the day?

What hypnotherapy for sleep can and can't do

Hypnotherapy is not a treatment for insomnia or other sleep disorders. For ongoing trouble falling or staying asleep, or symptoms such as loud snoring or waking gasping, talk with a healthcare professional. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a recommended treatment for chronic insomnia in adults. See the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health's sleep overview.

Hypnosis is often described as focused attention with relaxation; people experience it differently. Research on hypnotherapy for sleep has mixed results, so it should not be presented as a guaranteed way to sleep or as a substitute for appropriate care. In Ashley's personal-growth practice, a session may include a guided relaxation exercise and a conversation about habits or cues around winding down. Three gentle things to try tonight

These are low-pressure ideas, not treatment instructions. Skip anything that does not feel comfortable for you.

  1. Give the day an ending. Take a couple of minutes to write down something you want to remember tomorrow. You do not have to solve it tonight.
  2. Notice your breath. If comfortable, let the exhale be a little longer than the inhale for a few rounds. Return to your usual breathing if this feels uncomfortable.
  3. Choose one settling cue. Try dimming a light, setting your phone aside, or holding a warm cup while noticing its weight and temperature. Choose what feels kind and practical.

These ideas do not replace care for a sleep disorder. If sleep difficulties persist or affect your health and daily life, a healthcare professional can help you consider next steps.
